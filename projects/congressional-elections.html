Urbanization and Congressional Electoral Competitiveness

Are congressional districts with larger urban populations more or less electorally competitive? This project examines the relationship between urbanization, partisan winning margins, demographic composition, and congressional district boundaries.

Period: 1992–2024 Tools: SQL Server and Tableau Focus: U.S. House elections

Project Overview

Urban areas are generally associated with stronger Democratic electoral performance, particularly in major metropolitan areas such as New York City, Atlanta, and Chicago. Historically, the Democratic coalition had a broader rural presence, but as the economy and population have become increasingly urbanized, the geographic composition of the Democratic base has also shifted.

This changing political geography raises an important question: does the concentration of voters in urban areas translate into less competitive congressional districts?

Central finding: Urbanization alone does not explain electoral competitiveness. Highly urban districts can produce substantial winning margins for either party, while some moderately urban districts are considerably more competitive.

Data and Methodology

I used congressional election results from 1992 through 2024 to examine Democratic and Republican electoral performance across congressional districts. The analysis calculates total votes, party vote totals, winning parties, winning margins, and margin percentages.

I then connected the election results to Census congressional district data to examine the relationship between district population characteristics and electoral outcomes. Districts were compared across different levels of urbanization, allowing me to investigate patterns beyond a simple urban-versus-rural distinction.

Research process

  • Inspected and cleaned congressional election records.
  • Calculated Democratic and Republican vote totals and electoral winning margins.
  • Joined election results to congressional district Census demographic information.
  • Examined urban, rural, and total population characteristics.
  • Compared electoral competitiveness across states, urbanization categories, and election cycles.

    • District Selection and Geographic Coverage

    The district-level analysis uses a purposive sample of congressional districts across 15 states.

    Rather than randomly selecting districts, I reviewed congressional district maps and selected districts to examine a range of geographic and electoral contexts. The sample generally includes three districts per state, with at-large states represented by their single congressional district.

    The final election dataset contains 169 observations. However, 11 observations from North Dakota, South Dakota, and Vermont could not be matched to the Census urbanization data.

    These observations were retained in the broader election dataset but excluded from the urbanization analysis, which uses the 158 matched observations.

1. Are More Urban Districts Less Competitive?

Given the conventional understanding of urban partisan geography, I initially expected highly urban districts to be relatively safe Democratic seats. However, the data revealed a more complicated picture, including exceptions such as TX-2, CA-50, AZ-1, MI-3, and OK-1.

Urban populations are not politically homogeneous. Republicans live and vote in major cities, just as Democrats live and vote in rural communities. Urbanization describes the geographic characteristics of a population, not the political preferences of every individual within it. The way these populations are grouped into congressional districts can therefore have significant implications for electoral outcomes.

One of the most striking findings was the size of winning margins in districts with urban populations exceeding 90%. In 2016, the average Republican winning margin in this category was 29%, compared with 26% in 2024. Democratic winners had average margins of 41% in 2016 and 39% in 2024.

These figures suggest that highly urban districts can produce substantial winning margins for either party, although Democratic victories were particularly pronounced in this category.

This prompted me to examine district boundaries and consider the relationship between geographic concentration, redistricting, and electoral competition. In several districts I investigated, I noticed patterns that raised questions about partisan gerrymandering. Urban centers and communities that generally lean Democratic sometimes appeared concentrated within particular congressional districts, while Republican-leaning populations were also grouped in ways that could produce safe seats.

These arrangements can create districts with overwhelming winning margins for one party while reducing the competitiveness of surrounding districts. A district's electoral margin does not necessarily reflect the full political diversity of its surrounding region.

Electoral competitiveness alone cannot establish the effects of gerrymandering. Safe seats can reflect genuine political preferences as well as the geographic distribution of voters, but they can also be shaped by district design. When general elections become increasingly predictable, voters may have fewer opportunities to hold their representatives electorally accountable.

2. Competitiveness Across Levels of Urbanization

The relationship between urbanization and electoral competitiveness becomes more complicated when examining individual districts across the four urbanization categories.

The 50–74.99% urban category contained several particularly interesting examples. Oklahoma's third congressional district was a major outlier, with a 56-point Republican margin in 2016. Michigan's eighth district also demonstrated substantial electoral movement, shifting from a Republican victory by approximately 16.85 points in 2016 to a Democratic victory by approximately 6.6 points in 2024. Iowa's first district was another example, with a Republican victory by less than one point in 2024.

Georgia's twelfth congressional district also caught my attention. Despite having an urban population share of approximately 56%, the district maintained relatively substantial Republican margins in the elections examined. Its racial composition, college-educated population, and changing political environment raise questions about the relationship between demographic characteristics and electoral behavior.

More recent political developments in Georgia also make this district worth investigating further, particularly whether population growth, demographic change, and shifts in voter preferences are altering its electoral trajectory.

The less-than-50% urban category displayed a different pattern. Most of the districts examined produced Republican winning margins exceeding 35 points in the 2016 and 2024 observations, with North Carolina's first district standing out as a Democratic exception.

Across the categories, racial and demographic composition also appeared relevant. Districts with substantial Democratic margins were often heavily urban and had large Black or minority populations. For example, Texas's eighteenth congressional district has a substantial minority population and consistently produced large Democratic winning margins in the elections examined. Conversely, several predominantly white districts exhibited stronger Republican performance.

These observations suggest that urbanization alone cannot explain electoral outcomes. Race, educational attainment, geography, and other demographic characteristics may also help explain differences in electoral competitiveness. Demographics are potential indicators, not political destiny. Further research would be needed to understand how these characteristics interact.

3. Electoral Competitiveness Across Election Cycles

The time-series analysis revealed different patterns across urbanization categories. In the 90%+ urban category, average winning margins for both parties remained relatively stable between 2016 and 2024, with the two parties' lines appearing largely parallel.

The less-than-50% urban category showed a different trajectory, with Republican advantages becoming more pronounced in many of the districts examined. The middle urbanization categories displayed more variation, with the 50–74.99% category appearing particularly competitive across several election cycles.

The 1992, 2000, 2016, and 2024 observations were among the more competitive cycles in the 50–74.99% category. Average winning margins by party were:

  • 2024: Democrats 30.34%; Republicans 23.26%.
  • 2000: Democrats 28.81%; Republicans 25.26%.
  • 2016: Democrats 33.80%; Republicans 26.56%.
  • 1992: Democrats 25.59%; Republicans 23.66%.

Overall, the findings suggest that electoral competitiveness has evolved differently across levels of urbanization rather than following one consistent national pattern. They also raise a broader question about how much electoral behavior can be anticipated from the characteristics of a congressional district.

4. Redistricting, Voter Participation, and the Limits of Partisan Maps

North Carolina provides a particularly interesting example of the limits of partisan redistricting. Ahead of the 2026 midterm elections, Republican lawmakers redrew the state's congressional map in an effort to gain another Republican seat and make several districts more favorable to their party. However, the results were substantially different from what the map's designers had intended. Democrats flipped multiple Republican-held congressional districts, including seats that had been drawn to favor Republican candidates.

This occurred alongside unusually high voter participation. North Carolina recorded a 53.6% turnout rate, its highest in a non-presidential election since 1990, while early voting increased by 36% compared with 2022. However, turnout alone does not explain these results. The broader political environment, candidate quality, campaign organization, and changing voter preferences also contributed.

Rather than demonstrating that redistricting no longer matters, North Carolina illustrates that the electoral advantages created through district boundaries are not guaranteed. Mapmakers can shape the conditions of competition, but they cannot fully control how voters respond.

The results also raise questions about the relationship between electoral competition and campaign investment. When parties consider certain districts unwinnable, they may devote fewer resources to organizing, advertising, and campaigning there. This can create a cycle in which districts receive less attention precisely because their electoral outcomes are considered predictable.

Urban centers are particularly interesting in this regard. Republican campaigns may concentrate their efforts on suburban communities and metropolitan outskirts rather than compete as seriously for voters in city centers, while Democratic campaigns may make similar strategic calculations in heavily Republican areas.

I would be interested to see what congressional campaigns would look like if both parties had a genuine opportunity to compete across a wider range of urban, suburban, and rural districts. More competitive districts could encourage parties to invest in communities they might otherwise overlook, develop broader electoral coalitions, and give voters more opportunities to evaluate competing candidates and ideas.

This reinforces my interest in limiting partisan gerrymandering through independent or nonpartisan redistricting processes. Such reforms would not make every district competitive, nor would they eliminate the political differences between communities. However, they could reduce the ability of political mapmakers to predetermine electoral advantages through district boundaries.

5. Limitations and Further Research

An important limitation is that the urbanization classifications use contemporary Census district characteristics rather than historical population data for each election cycle. Population composition and congressional boundaries have changed over time, meaning the current urbanization classifications may not accurately reflect the historical characteristics of every district.

The analysis also identifies relationships between district characteristics and electoral outcomes, rather than establishing that any single demographic characteristic causes a particular voting pattern. Urbanization, race, education, geography, district boundaries, and changing political coalitions may interact in ways that require further investigation.

Future research could examine how redistricting changes electoral competitiveness, whether campaign investment varies across districts with different levels of competitiveness, and how demographic shifts affect partisan coalitions over time.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that urbanization is associated with electoral competitiveness, but it is not sufficient to explain partisan outcomes. Highly urban districts frequently produce substantial winning margins, while some moderately urban districts exhibit greater electoral competition. Less urban districts in the sample have generally demonstrated stronger Republican advantages in more recent observations.

These patterns raise broader questions about the relationship between partisan geography, demographic change, redistricting, and congressional representation. Electoral competition is an important component of representative democracy. I am particularly interested in how district boundaries can shape the opportunities voters have to influence representation, and whether reducing partisan manipulation in redistricting could create more meaningful electoral competition.

Ultimately, this analysis demonstrates that the relationship between geography and electoral behavior is more complicated than a simple urban-versus-rural divide. It also points toward further questions about how district boundaries, demographic change, and shifting political coalitions shape the opportunities parties have to compete.
