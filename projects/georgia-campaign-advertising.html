Campaign Advertising in Georgia's 2026 Governor's Race

How did Rick Jackson and Keisha Lance Bottoms present themselves to voters? This project examines 20 campaign advertisements, looking at themes, candidate presentation, emotional appeal, and the central arguments each campaign made.

Sample: 20 advertisements (10 per candidate) Method: Qualitative ad coding Focus: 2026 Georgia gubernatorial race

Project Overview

I hate to beat a dead horse, but Georgia's political environment makes a strong association with Donald Trump an interesting campaign choice. By October 2026, Trump's net approval rating in Georgia had fallen to -26, according to Civiqs' rolling tracker of registered voters. An October AJC/UGA poll also found that 55% of likely Georgia voters disapproved of his job performance, compared with 42% who approved. With that backdrop, I was interested in how the candidates approached Trump's influence in their campaign advertising.

I vividly remember visiting home during the Republican primary and seeing what felt like a Rick Jackson advertisement every three minutes on my grandmother's television. Each time one came on, I found myself thinking that something about the messaging seemed off. I also remember commenting on how rarely I encountered Keisha Lance Bottoms (KLB) advertisements in comparison. News about Bottoms' fundraising and Jackson's substantial advertising expenditures only made me more curious about how their campaigns were presenting themselves to voters.

With the post-election rush finally settling, I decided to watch and analyze 20 campaign advertisements, 10 from Jackson and 10 from Bottoms. I classified each advertisement by its main themes, candidate presentation, message type, emotional appeal, intended audience, and central argument. Rather than simply comparing how often each candidate advertised, I wanted to examine what they were actually communicating to voters and how they used their advertisements to make their case.

Keisha Lance Bottoms

With Keisha Lance Bottoms, one of the first things I noticed was how consistently her advertisements returned to a message of hope. Particularly after the first debate, her campaign's social media and advertising seemed to lean into a familiar kind of hopeful, forward-looking messaging, reminiscent of the optimism associated with Kamala Harris's campaign. The message was simple: Georgia could be better, a better future was possible, and she would stand up for the people who felt they deserved more.

When watching her advertisements, I often walked away with a feeling of cautious optimism. In This May Be Funny, especially, Bottoms came across as natural, personable, and almost like someone you could sit down and have lunch with. There was a certain ease to her presentation that made the message feel conversational rather than overly rehearsed. That distinction mattered because her advertisements were not just communicating policy positions; they were also building a particular impression of who she was as a candidate.

Of the 10 advertisements I watched, nine centered on hope for Georgia's future and touched on issues that affect people's everyday lives, including the economy, housing affordability, wages, groceries, and healthcare. Across the sample, her messaging consistently emphasized advocating for Georgians, defending their interests, and improving their quality of life. Although she did use contrast and attack messaging in some advertisements, it did not appear to be the primary focus of the sample. More often, she returned to what she wanted to do for the state rather than making Rick Jackson the center of her message.

Initially, I wondered whether I was reading too much into the advertisements because Bottoms was the candidate I preferred in the race. However, after watching the ads, I began noticing similar reactions and interpretations in social media discussions, including Reddit threads, TikToks, and posts on X. That did not make my observations representative of Georgia voters, but it did make me more interested in how other people were interpreting her campaign's messaging. What stood out to me was the consistency: regardless of the issue being discussed, the advertisements repeatedly returned to a hopeful vision of what Georgia could become.

Rick Jackson

I approached Rick Jackson's advertisements with a level of curiosity I had never really experienced in a Georgia gubernatorial race. In my lifetime, I had not encountered a candidate quite like him. I knew going in that he was a billionaire, and after seeing his frequently aired advertisement with Brian Kemp, I understood that he had the resources to fund his own campaign. Naturally, I went into the advertisements with high expectations for both messaging and production value. However, when it came to the messaging, I often found myself underwhelmed.

Like Keisha Lance Bottoms, Jackson also offered themes of hope, but they came across differently. Much of his advertising relied on his personal upbringing: growing up in the projects, having an alcoholic mother, being abandoned by his father, running away as a child, having seven stepfathers, and eventually becoming a billionaire. His story certainly carried emotional weight. However, I often felt that the storytelling stopped at the point of his personal success. The advertisements showed how he had overcome difficult circumstances, but rarely explained what that experience meant for his approach to governing, what policies he intended to pursue, or how it qualified him to lead Georgia.

I coded nearly nine of his 10 advertisements as having a hopeful emotional appeal. This was largely because of the repeated emphasis on overcoming hardship and the possibility of making it out of difficult circumstances. Yet there was a noticeable gap between the personal story being told and the future being promised to Georgians. The advertisements often suggested that because Jackson had overcome adversity, he understood the struggles of ordinary people. What I found myself looking for was the next step: what exactly would he do to make life better for them?

There were, however, a couple of advertisements that moved beyond the themes I had come to associate with Jackson's campaign. One featured his grandchildren and took a more personal, family-oriented approach. What made the advertisement particularly interesting was Jackson's acknowledgment that his grandchildren thought his other advertisements were scary. In a way, the advertisement seemed to recognize the contrast between the image he had built through much of his campaign advertising and the more personal side he wanted voters to see. I ultimately coded it as "Unsure" because I finished watching without a particularly clear understanding of the message it was trying to communicate.

This also reminded me of Bottoms' use of her own children in her advertisements. Both candidates incorporated family into their public presentation, but the execution felt different to me. Bottoms' children appeared as part of a broader portrayal of her leadership and personal life, and their participation came across as more natural and conversational. Jackson's advertisement, by comparison, felt more explicitly like an attempt to soften the image established by his other advertisements. It was a small but interesting difference in how the two campaigns attempted to make their candidates relatable to voters.

Another notable example was Foster, which highlighted Jackson's work helping more than 800 foster youth attend college. This was one of the more concrete accomplishments presented in his advertisements and offered viewers something beyond his personal upbringing to consider. The advertisement also stood out when compared with Bottoms' approach to education. While Jackson highlighted his personal involvement in helping foster youth access college and spoke about the importance of quality education, Bottoms discussed fully funding education and presented a broader vision for the state's education system. The distinction was not that one candidate discussed education and the other did not, but that they approached the issue from different directions. Jackson emphasized an accomplishment from his personal life, while Bottoms more directly communicated what she wanted to pursue through state policy.

The remaining advertisement I coded around frustration repeatedly invoked Donald Trump and the idea that Trump "wins, wins, and wins." Across multiple advertisements, Jackson also repeatedly described himself as an outsider, drawing a parallel between his own political identity and Trump's. This was an interesting contrast given his background as a billionaire and his access to substantial campaign resources. The outsider identity appeared to be an important part of how he wanted voters to understand him: someone outside the political establishment who could challenge the people already in power. Beyond that appeal to Trump's political identity and record, the clearest policy proposal I recall from Jackson's advertisements was lowering property taxes. This left me with a relatively short list of identifiable messages despite watching all 10 advertisements.

Another recurring element was the criticism of Bottoms' record as mayor of Atlanta, particularly surrounding crime and policing. Jackson's advertisements repeatedly invoked the unrest of 2020, portraying Atlanta as a city that had been allowed to descend into chaos under her leadership. However, I found the portrayal worth examining more closely. Atlanta did experience a significant increase in violent crime during the pandemic. Homicides increased 59% between 2019 and 2020, compared with an average increase of 33% among 70 large police agencies. The destruction surrounding the protests was also real, including a burned Wendy's, a police vehicle, and other damaged property. However, the campaign's repeated imagery of a city burning presented a broader picture than the actual destruction.

I was also struck by the way the advertisements characterized Bottoms' relationship with law enforcement. During her administration, she authorized a 30% pay increase for police officers, supported additional police recruitment and retention efforts, increased public safety spending, and advanced plans for a new public safety training academy. Her record also included police reform initiatives and efforts to redirect some corrections spending toward community services. That record is more complicated than the suggestion that she was simply opposed to law enforcement. The advertisements largely left out that complexity, instead presenting her mayoralty through the lens of crime, unrest, and public safety failures.

This also revealed an interesting difference in how the two campaigns approached public safety. Jackson's campaign used the issue primarily as an attack on Bottoms' record, while Bottoms' campaign generally used public safety as an opportunity to present herself as an experienced leader. In the advertisements I reviewed, the distinction was not simply whether the candidates discussed crime and policing, but how they used those issues to make their case to voters. Jackson emphasized what he believed Bottoms had done wrong, while Bottoms more often emphasized the leadership and experience she believed she could bring to the state.

Conclusion

By the time I finished reviewing Jackson's advertisements and organizing my notes, I found myself struggling to summarize what I had learned about his campaign beyond a few recurring ideas: a difficult upbringing, lower property taxes, an association with Donald Trump, and a handful of personal accomplishments. That is not to say Jackson lacked a broader policy agenda outside the advertisements I watched. Rather, it speaks to what his campaign chose to communicate through this particular sample. For a candidate with the resources to communicate an extensive vision for Georgia, I had expected to come away with a much clearer understanding of what he wanted to accomplish as governor.

Ultimately, what stood out to me was the difference between telling voters who you are and telling them what you intend to do for them. Jackson's advertisements gave considerable attention to his personal journey and the obstacles he had overcome, but I often found myself searching for the connection between that story and his vision for Georgia. Even when the advertisements moved toward education, public safety, or affordability, I wanted more detail about what those priorities would look like in practice. By the end of the exercise, I understood considerably more about Jackson's upbringing than I did about the policies he intended to pursue as governor.
